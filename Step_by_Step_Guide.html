FCBEM Study 1 · plain-language guide

From Comments to Crossover — a Step-by-Step Guide

How to take thousands of Vietnamese Facebook comments about a virtual influencer and turn them into one honest answer: when did the audience stop treating her as a “fake thing” and start treating her as a person — and did shopping talk follow? Written so that someone with no statistics background can follow every step and understand why it is done.

Companion to VI_Acceptance_Playbook_FCBEM.html (which has the formal details, code and references). Prepared 2026-08-21.

The idea in one picture

Imagine a new student who joins your class but looks slightly “off” — maybe a very realistic robot. In week 1 everyone whispers “that's creepy, look at the eyes”. By week 10 people say “she's actually funny” and “hey, are you coming tomorrow?”. By week 20 someone asks “where did you get those shoes?”. We want to measure exactly that journey for a virtual influencer (VI), using what people write in public comments.

What you need before starting

  • The comments — already crawled into data/db/scraper.sqlite (≈ 5,900 comments for Tiểu Mỹ; more personas coming).
  • Python (pandas) for cleaning and for talking to the AI; R (packages mgcv, gratia, segmented) for the curves.
  • A DeepSeek API key (the model is DeepSeek V4 Flash; the whole project costs a few dollars).
  • Two Vietnamese-speaking people willing to label 600–800 comments by hand. This is the most important ingredient.
Step 1

Clean the comments

WhyMessy input gives wrong answers. The crawler sometimes captured the same comment twice, picked up Facebook's image descriptions as if they were comments, and the VI's own account replies to fans.
How
  • Remove exact duplicates (same post + same author + same text).
  • Remove rows that are really image alt-text (they start with “May be an image of…” or “Avatar is pointing…”).
  • Remove comments written by the VI's own account.
  • Mark (don't delete) emoji-only comments, obvious spam (“Follow”, repeated copy-paste, links) and non-Vietnamese comments. We keep them for counting but not for labelling.
  • Replace each author's name/id with a random code (hash). We never need real identities.
What you getOne clean table, one row per comment, with columns: comment id, post id, persona, text, author code, time, is-reply, parent comment text, post caption.
Step 2

Give every comment a week number

WhyThe whole study is about change over time, so every comment needs a position on a timeline that is the same for every persona: weeks since that persona's first post (week 0 = launch).
How
  • Facebook shows some times exactly (2026-05-12 16:21) and many only roughly (“13 weeks ago”). For rough ones, subtract from the moment the crawler ran: crawl date − 13 weeks. That is accurate to about ± 1 week — fine for weekly analysis, and we will double-check later with 2-week bins.
  • Decide the launch date for each persona (first public post) and write it down in a small file personas.csv.
  • week_since_launch = (comment date − launch date) ÷ 7, rounded down.
What you getColumns comment_time, time_precision (exact / day / week) and week_since_launch. For Tiểu Mỹ that is weeks 0–23.

Watch out: one viral post holds 23% of all comments, and some weeks have only 3 comments while one week has 2,278. That is why, later, we will not just compute “% per week” — we will use a model that knows which post each comment belongs to.

Step 3

Decide exactly what you are measuring

Why“Is the comment positive or negative?” is not the question. The question is how does the commenter relate to the VI. A comment can be negative and still treat her like a normal influencer (“Con nhỏ này hôm nay lại đi quảng cáo nữa à =)))”). So we measure four things, and a comment can be several at once.
The four labels (each is yes/no)
LabelMeans the comment…Real examples
uncanny resistancefocuses on her looking/sounding fake, strange, creepy; rejects her because she's artificial“Al hát ghép nên nghe k thật” · “mỗi lúc 1 khác chả thấy đồng nhất”
AI skepticismis against AI in general or distrusts brands using AI — not about how she looks“sống chung với rác AI”
parasocial / relationaltalks to her or about her like a person: calls her chị/em/bé, likes her humour, asks her to post more, defends her, jokes with her — even if teasing“Sắp thuộc rồi chú Khoa ơi” · “nói AI bé nó buồn đấy”
commercial receptivityasks about a product, price, link, brand; wants to try/buy; likes a collab“Quạt, quạt gì cơ” · “xin link”

Plus two helpers: sentiment (negative / neutral / positive) and aspects — which part of her is being judged (appearance, voice/motion, being-AI, personality, the content, the product) and whether it is praised or criticised. The aspects are what let a manager read “appearance complaints faded but voice complaints stayed”.

What you getA clear list of label definitions. Nothing coded yet.
Step 4

Write the rulebook (the codebook) with a small pilot

WhyTwo people reading “parasocial” will disagree unless the rule says exactly what counts. Vietnamese slang makes this harder. A written rulebook with real examples is what makes the labels scientific instead of opinions.
How
  • Pick 200 comments — but not randomly. Randomly you would get mostly “xinh quá” and emojis; creepy-talk is only ~2% and shopping-talk ~3%. So pick a mix: every persona, every month, every content type, and deliberately include comments containing words like giả, ghê, AI, đơ, link, mua, giá.
  • Trúc and Minh label them together, argue about the hard ones, and write down the decisions: “AI hả?” (just a question) is not resistance; “lại quảng cáo nữa” is relational, not commercial; teasing counts as relational.
  • Note any new pattern that keeps appearing (e.g. joking as if she were a real girlfriend) — these become “emergent themes” in the paper.
What you getCodebook v1: for every label a definition, what to include, what to exclude, and 3–5 Vietnamese examples.
Step 5

Build the answer key (gold set) with two human coders

WhyThe AI will label everything later, but we can only trust it if we can check it against humans. The gold set is the answer key for that exam.
How
  • Pick 600–800 new comments the same balanced way as the pilot.
  • Two Vietnamese coders label them independently (no talking), using the codebook, reading the raw Vietnamese (never a translation).
  • Measure how much they agree with Krippendorff's α: it is “agreement beyond what luck would give”, from 0 (pure luck) to 1 (perfect). We want ≥ 0.67 per label, ideally ≥ 0.80. If a label is low, the codebook rule for it is unclear — fix the rule, re-label that part.
  • Where they disagree, they discuss and pick the final answer.
  • Split the gold set: about 40% becomes the practice set (for tuning the AI) and 60% the exam set (touched once, at the end).
What you getA file with ~700 comments, final human labels, the α numbers, and a column saying practice/exam.
Step 6

Teach the AI and give it the exam

WhyHumans can label 700 comments; they cannot label 30,000. DeepSeek can — cheaply — but only if we prove it agrees with humans.
How
  • Write the instructions (the “prompt”): the role, the codebook word-for-word, and 8–12 solved examples taken from the practice set. Ask for a strict JSON answer per comment, e.g. {"uv_resistance":1,"parasocial_orientation":1,"commercial_acceptance":1,"sentiment":"pos", ...}. Use temperature 0 (no randomness). Record the model name and date.
  • Run it on the practice set, look at the mistakes, improve the wording. Repeat a few times — on the practice set only.
  • Run it once on the exam set and score it with:
    • Precision — of the comments the AI called “resistance”, how many really were? (Did it cry wolf?)
    • Recall — of the real “resistance” comments, how many did the AI find? (Did it miss some?)
    • F1 — the balance of the two. Macro-F1 — the average over all labels so rare labels count as much as common ones.
    Never report only “accuracy”: 97% of comments are not commercial, so an AI that says “never commercial” is 97% accurate and useless.
  • Re-run 5% of comments a second time to show the AI gives the same answer twice (aim ≥ 90%).
What you getA validation table for the paper: α per label, precision/recall/F1 per label, macro-F1, and the confusion matrix in the appendix.
Step 7

Label everything

HowFreeze the prompt. Send every cleaned comment (with its post caption and parent comment for context) to DeepSeek; save the JSON answers into the table. Keep the prompt, model name, date and settings in the paper's appendix so anyone can repeat it.
What you getThe full analysis table: one row per comment with week, post, persona, the four labels, sentiment, aspects.
Step 8

Draw the weekly curves — properly

WhyThe tempting thing is “% of comments with resistance, per week” and connect the dots. Two problems: (1) one viral post would dominate its week; (2) if the VI's graphics improved over time, negative comments could fall for that reason, not because people got used to her. We need a method that handles both.
How (the idea, no maths)
  • Use a smooth-curve model (a GAM, in R's mgcv). Think of it as drawing the best-fitting smooth line through the weekly data, with a shaded band showing how unsure we are. It does not force a straight line — it can fall fast, flatten, then fall again.
  • Tell the model which post each comment belongs to and which persona — so a giant viral post counts as one noisy event, not as the whole truth of that week.
  • Add controls per post: sponsored or not, content type, how realistic the video looks, whether AI was disclosed. This is what lets us say “the audience changed” rather than “the videos got better”.
  • Do it three times: for resistance R(t), relational S(t), commercial C(t). Also compute the slope of each curve (how fast it falls/rises) — that is the “resistance decay rate” and “socialization growth rate” in the paper.
What you getFigure 1 — three curves with bands; and the answers to H1 (resistance falls) and H2 (relational rises), with confidence intervals.
Step 9

Find the crossover point — and how sure you are

WhyThe paper's headline is “the week relational talk durably overtook creepy talk”. A single week where the lines happen to touch is noise. And “exactly day 76” sounds precise but isn't.
How
  • Compute the gap D(t) = S(t) − R(t). Below zero: resistance still dominates. Above zero: relational talk dominates.
  • Rule, decided in advance: the crossover week τ* is the first week where D(t) is above zero and stays above zero for 3 weeks in a row, using the lower edge of the confidence band (so it is really above, not maybe). Write this rule in the paper before you see the result.
  • How sure? Use a bootstrap: put all the posts in a hat, draw posts at random with replacement to make a new dataset of the same size, redo steps 8–9, note τ*. Do this 1,000 times. The middle 95% of those τ* values is your confidence interval. Report “τ* ≈ week 14, 95% CI [12, 18]”.
  • If the lines never cross in the window, say so: “resistance fell from .18 to .05 but relational talk did not overtake it within 23 weeks.” That is a real, publishable result — never force a crossover.
  • Optional check: a breakpoint test (R segmented) asks the data how many “phases” it actually supports — one bend, two bends, none.
What you getFigure 2 (the gap curve crossing zero), τ* with its interval, one per persona (Figure 3), and the phase count.
Step 10

Does shopping talk follow?

WhyH3/H4 say: when the balance tips toward relational talk, commercial talk rises afterwards.
HowMake a weekly table (one row per week per persona): share of commercial comments this week, the gap D last week, commercial share last week, how many sponsored/product posts the VI made this week, how many comments there were. Fit a simple regression: this week's commercial share explained by last week's gap, controlling for the rest. A positive, significant coefficient on “last week's gap” supports H4. Also compare average commercial share before vs after τ* — but call it a description, not a cause (τ* came from the same data).
What you getFigure 7 and one coefficient with its interval.
Step 11

Stress-test the result

WhyA reviewer will ask “would you get the same answer if you had chosen differently?”. Answer before they ask.
Re-run steps 8–10 with
  • 2-week bins instead of weekly (because of the ±1-week timestamps);
  • only the human-labelled comments (does the AI change the story?);
  • spam and non-Vietnamese removed;
  • the viral post (1,357 comments) removed;
  • each persona alone; sponsored posts vs organic posts separately;
  • a strict vs broad definition of “resistance”;
  • the crossover rule with 2 or 4 weeks instead of 3;
  • only posts where we collected ≥ 50% of the comments Facebook shows.
If τ* moves a little, fine. If it appears only under one choice, say the evidence is weak.
Step 12

Tell the story with figures, then say what you can and cannot claim

The figures, in order
  1. Three curves with bands and τ* shaded — “resistance fades, relationship grows, the balance tips here”.
  2. The gap curve crossing zero — “and it stays tipped”.
  3. One small panel per persona — “speed differs by persona”.
  4. Aspect heatmap (rows: appearance, voice, AI-ness, persona, content, product; columns: weeks; colour: praise minus criticism) — “what exactly people stopped complaining about”.
  5. Relational share vs positive-sentiment share, early vs late — “normal is not the same as liked”.
  6. Repeat commenters' first → last label (Sankey) — “individuals move the same way”.
  7. Commercial share with τ* marked + the coefficient — “shopping talk follows”.
Plus tables: corpus description; validation numbers; how often labels co-occur (many people mock the face and ask for the link in one comment); example comments per label and phase, in Vietnamese with an English gloss.
What you may claimMarket-level discourse normalization and its timing; a lagged association with commercial-receptivity talk; normalization without positivity; a practical leading indicator for campaign timing.
What you may not claimThat individual consumers changed their minds (different people each week), that the crossover causes purchases, or that the uncanny valley “disappears by itself” everywhere.

Glossary

Virtual influencer (VI)
A computer-generated persona that posts like a human influencer.
Uncanny valley
The creepy feeling when something looks almost — but not quite — human.
Parasocial
A one-sided relationship where a viewer feels they know a media figure personally.
Codebook
The written rulebook that says exactly what each label means, with examples.
Gold set
Comments labelled by humans; the answer key for checking the AI.
Krippendorff's α
How much two coders agree beyond chance (0 = chance, 1 = perfect).
Precision / Recall / F1
Did the AI cry wolf? / Did it miss cases? / The balance of both.
GAM
A model that fits a smooth curve (not forced straight) with an uncertainty band.
Bootstrap
Re-draw the data many times to see how much a result would wobble.
Crossover point τ*
The first week relational talk durably exceeds resistance talk.
Robustness check
Redoing the analysis with different reasonable choices to see if the answer holds.
