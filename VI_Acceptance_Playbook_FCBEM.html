1. The business question and the one figure the paper stands on

Brands in Vietnam are already using AI-generated personas. The managerial problem is timing and risk: when does an audience stop talking about a virtual influencer as a “fake thing” and start talking about her as a persona they relate to — and does commercial receptivity follow? The paper's contribution is a measurable, time-stamped answer: the Normalization Crossover Point (τ*), the week in which relational (parasocial) talk durably overtakes uncanny/artificiality talk in public discourse, and whether product-related talk rises after it.

Everything below is in service of producing Figure 1 credibly for a marketing reviewer: a transparent sample, constructs grounded in consumer theory (uncanny valley, mere exposure, parasocial interaction, source credibility), coding validated by Vietnamese human coders, and statistics that state uncertainty honestly.

2. Data collection — documented for the Methodology section

This section is written so it can be lifted, trimmed, and pasted into 3.1 Research design and 3.2 Instrumentation. Numbers refer to the corpus as of 2026-08-21.

2.1 Research setting and sampling frame

  • Platform. Facebook — the dominant social platform in Vietnam and the channel on which Vietnamese virtual influencers (VIs) publish and receive public comments. Study unit hierarchy: persona → post/reel → public comment/reply.
  • Personas. Vietnamese-language, persistently active VI pages. Collected so far: Tiểu Mỹ (facebook.com/tieumyday), an AI-generated singer/persona with both organic (daily-life, music) and product-oriented (e.g., “Lần đầu bán quạt…”) posts. Target: 4–6 personas with different levels of human-likeness and different launch dates, so that curves can be compared across personas and the result is not one persona's story.
  • Observation window. All public posts from page launch (time zero, t = 0) to collection date. For Tiểu Mỹ: 2026-03-10 → 2026-08-19 (23 weeks).
  • Unit of analysis. The public comment (top-level or reply). Unit of time: week since launch (2-week bins as sensitivity).
  • Inclusion. Vietnamese-language public comments on the persona's own posts/reels. Exclusion: comments posted by the persona account itself, spam/bot content, non-Vietnamese text, platform alt-text artefacts.

2.2 Collection instrument

Comments were collected with a purpose-built browser-automation crawler (Python, Playwright on Chromium) that reproduces what a logged-in user sees on the public page — it does not use private APIs or access non-public content. The procedure per persona:

  1. Timeline discovery. Scroll the page feed and the Reels tab; record every post/reel with its feed position, caption, publication date, reactions, shares and Facebook's displayed comment count (used later as a coverage denominator).
  2. Post opening. Open each post (preferably by permalink — this path returns full, absolute timestamps and stable author identifiers in 97–100% of cases, versus 15–34% via the feed lightbox).
  3. Thread expansion. Switch the comment sort to “All comments”, repeatedly expand “View more comments / replies” up to a per-post cap (600 expansion rounds or 15 minutes), and read comment text, author, author id, timestamp, parent relation and content type from the rendered DOM.
  4. Persistence & resumability. Each post is written to SQLite (data/db/scraper.sqlite) as soon as it is done; runs can be resumed and are merged by union, so repeated runs deepen coverage rather than overwrite.
  5. Coverage measurement. For each post, collected comments are compared to the displayed comment badge (ui_comment_count); the gap is stored per post and used in robustness analyses.

2.4 Data cleaning rules (pre-registered in the paper)

  • Remove exact duplicates (same post + author + text) and comments by the persona's own account (35).
  • Remove Facebook alt-text artefacts captured as text (“May be an image of…”, “Avatar is pointing…”).
  • Flag (not delete) emoji-only comments — they count toward engagement volume but carry no construct label.
  • Flag suspected spam: single-word “Follow”, identical text repeated by one author across posts, links.
  • Flag non-Vietnamese text with a language detector set permissively (teencode confuses detectors); exclude in a robustness run.
  • Hash author identifiers before analysis; keep only the hash for repeat-commenter analysis.

2.5 Ethics and limitations statement (draft wording)

Data consist of publicly visible comments on public brand/persona pages, collected for non-commercial academic research; no private messages, friend-only content, or profile data were accessed. Author identifiers were pseudonymised by one-way hashing before analysis and no individual is identified in reporting. Collection reproduces an ordinary logged-in browsing session and respects platform rate limits; comments on highly viral posts are under-sampled (50–60% coverage), which we address through coverage-based robustness checks. Timestamps shown by the platform as relative (“N weeks ago”) were converted to calendar weeks with ±1-week precision.

Mention, don't hide: automated collection from Facebook sits in a grey zone of the platform's terms even when only public content is read. Marketing journals generally accept it with a clear ethics statement and pseudonymisation; say it explicitly in Limitations rather than leaving a reviewer to raise it.

3. What we measure: from “sentiment” to four consumer responses

A sentiment score answers “is the audience happy?”. Your research questions ask something more specific and more useful to managers: what kind of relationship does the audience have with the persona, and is it moving toward commercial readiness? Two observations from the corpus show why sentiment alone misleads:

  • “Con nhỏ này hôm nay lại đi quảng cáo nữa à =)))” — mildly negative, yet the commenter treats the VI exactly like any human influencer. That is normalization without positivity.
  • “biết rằng tạo bằng AI nhưng ngoài thực tìm ra được em này không ta” — artificiality acknowledged, yet the response is attraction and curiosity. Positive sentiment, still artificiality-focused.

So the primary measurement is four consumer-response constructs, each coded present/absent, several allowed per comment. Sentiment is kept as a secondary, descriptive variable.

Naming for the paper. Call the downstream construct commercial receptivity / commercial intent signal, not purchase intention — purchase intention is a psychometric construct measured by survey (e.g., Dabiran et al., 2024), and reviewers will object if a comment is labelled as intention. Call the phenomenon market-level discourse normalization, because different people comment in different weeks; the repeat-commenter subsample is the only within-person evidence.

4. Coding at scale: AI-assisted content analysis with human validation

Marketing journals now accept large language models as coders provided the workflow is human-led and validated on held-out human-coded data (Li et al., 2024, Marketing Science; Arora et al., 2025, Journal of Marketing; Carlson & Burbano, 2026, SMJ; Törnberg, 2024). This is standard content analysis (Krippendorff, 2018) with the LLM replacing the third, fourth… coder. For Vietnamese specifically, zero-shot LLM sentiment accuracy is only moderate (GPT-4 F1 ≈ .67 on Vietnamese benchmarks — Truong et al., 2024), which is exactly why the human gold standard is the centre of the method, not a formality.

You have chosen DeepSeek V4 Flash: open-weight, cheap (the whole corpus of 5 personas costs well under US$5), and version-pinnable — which also answers the reproducibility concern raised about closed models (Ollion et al., 2024).

4.1 The workflow (write this as Section 3.2 Instrumentation)

  1. Clean & time-resolve the corpus (Section 2.4); attach to each comment the post caption, content type, sponsorship flag, and the parent comment if it is a reply.
  2. Pilot coding (humans, ~200 comments). Stratified by persona × month × content type, oversampling comments that contain resistance or product vocabulary so the rare categories appear. Output: the codebook — definition, include/exclude rules, 3–5 raw Vietnamese examples per code, and documented emergent themes (e.g., joking anthropomorphism, ironic “she's advertising again” talk).
  3. Gold standard (two Vietnamese coders, 600–800 comments). Independent coding on raw Vietnamese; disagreements adjudicated. Report Krippendorff's α per construct (≥ .67 acceptable, ≥ .80 good) and Cohen's κ.
  4. Freeze; split gold into development (≈ 40%) and test (≈ 60%). The prompt is tuned on development only; the test set is touched once.
  5. LLM coding. One comment per request (or ≤ 25 per batch with ids echoed), temperature 0, JSON output; record model id, date, prompt, parameters. Re-code a 5% sample to report the model's self-consistency.
  6. Evaluation on test. Per construct: precision, recall, F1, confusion matrix; macro-F1 overall. Target recall ≥ .70 on the rare constructs. Never report accuracy alone — with 97% of comments not commercial, “predict none” scores 97%.
  7. Code the full corpus with the frozen prompt. Then carry coding uncertainty forward: (a) repeat the main analysis on the human-coded subset and show the same direction; (b) bootstrap by post; (c) optionally apply the design-based correction of Egami et al. (2023).

The full codebook prompt is in Appendix A. It is multi-label (a comment can be resistance + relational + commercial at once, e.g. “Nhìn mặt vẫn hơi giả nhưng bà này nói chuyện hài ghê, cái áo đẹp xin link”), which is what later lets you show coexistence — a finding in itself.

5. Aspect-level reading: what consumers reject and what they embrace

To give managers actionable detail, add an aspect-based sentiment layer: for each comment, which aspect of the VI is being judged and with what valence. Use a fixed set of aspect categories (the approach used in Vietnamese ABSA benchmarks — UIT-ViSFD, VLSP-2018 — and in SemEval), not free-text aspect extraction, which breaks on teencode.

AspectCoversManagerial reading
APPEARANCEface, eyes, skin, body, visual realismDesign/rendering quality; the classic uncanny trigger
VOICE_MOTIONvoice, lip-sync, singing, movementProduction quality; often the last mismatch to disappear
AI_NATUREbeing AI per se, AI in societyDisclosure/transparency strategy; ethical positioning
PERSONApersonality, humour, charm, relationship talkCharacter strategy — this is where relational value is built
CONTENTthe song/video/story itselfContent strategy (control)
PRODUCT_BRANDproduct, price, link, collab, adCommercial readiness and fit of the sponsorship

Each aspect receives negative / neutral / positive. The insight is the aspect × week net-polarity heatmap: managers see appearance complaints fading, persona praise rising, product curiosity appearing — and can read which lever (rendering, character, disclosure, product fit) is moving. It also yields a second, independent crossover estimate (the week PERSONA:positive overtakes APPEARANCE:negative + VOICE_MOTION:negative); if it falls inside the interval of the construct-based τ*, you have convergent evidence.

6. Analysis: trajectories, crossover, and commercial receptivity

Written for a business reader first; model specifications are in Appendix B. Tools: Python for cleaning and LLM coding; R (mgcv, segmented, boot) for modelling.

6.1 Step 1 — Trajectories (RQ1, RQ2; H1, H2)

For each construct, estimate the probability that a comment posted in week t carries that construct, with a flexible curve over time rather than a straight line (mere-exposure effects are often non-linear — Montoya et al., 2017), while controlling for what the persona posted (sponsored vs organic, content type, visual realism, AI disclosure) and for the fact that comments cluster inside posts and personas. The method is a generalized additive model with random effects (Wood, 2017; Pedersen et al., 2019): it produces smooth curves with confidence bands, and the rate of change (resistance decay rate, socialization growth rate) comes directly from the slope of those curves (Simpson, 2018).

Why the controls matter to the story: if the VI's graphics, voice and storytelling improve over six months, negative talk can fall because the product got better, not because the audience normalized. Coding realism and sponsorship per post is what lets you attribute the change to audience adaptation.

6.2 Step 2 — The crossover point (RQ3)

Compute the difference curve D(t) = S(t) − R(t). Define τ* before looking at the result: the earliest week at which the lower 95% bound of D(t) is above zero for at least three consecutive weeks (a pre-registered decision rule, so one noisy week cannot create a “crossover”). Quantify uncertainty by re-sampling posts (cluster bootstrap, 500–1,000 replications; Cameron et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2015) and re-estimating τ* each time. Report “τ* ≈ week 14, 95% CI [12, 18]” — or “no sustained crossover within the 23-week window; resistance fell from .18 to .05 while relational talk rose from .12 to .21”. Both are publishable; do not force it.

6.3 Step 3 — Phase structure (robustness)

Your narrative proposes phases (visual resistance → social reframing → commercial accommodation). Test rather than assume it: segmented regression with data-estimated breakpoints (Muggeo, 2003, 2008) tells you whether the weekly series supports zero, one or two breakpoints. If one, the paper reports two phases.

6.4 Step 4 — Commercial receptivity (RQ4; H3, H4)

Two versions of RQ4, depending on the persona. For personas without brand partnerships (e.g. Tiểu Mỹ): C(t) measures spontaneous commercial receptivity — report as exploratory, drop the sponsored-share control (no variance), keep commercial_post level as a covariate. For personas with brand-tied posts: the full H3/H4 test applies. A persona with no commercial stimulus is also a clean baseline of normalization uncontaminated by advertising, which lets you answer “does sponsorship accelerate or delay normalization?” by comparing curves across personas.

Relate weekly commercial receptivity Ct to the previous week's balance Dt−1, controlling for how much sponsored/product content the persona posted that week and for last week's C. A positive, significant coefficient supports “a more favourable relational-to-resistance balance precedes more commercial-receptivity talk”. The before/after-τ* comparison of C is descriptive; because τ* is estimated from the same data it is not a causal interrupted-time-series design (Bernal et al., 2017) — say so.

6.5 Step 5 — Repeat commenters (Layer B)

346 commenters appear three or more times. Order their comments in time and tabulate first-label → last-label transitions (resistance → relational → commercial). It is a selective group, so present it as supporting within-person evidence, not the main result.

8. What the paper can and cannot claim

You can say

  • In Vietnamese public discourse, uncanny-related resistance toward VI X attenuated over N weeks while relational talk grew; the balance tipped at τ* [CI] (or: did not tip within the window).
  • A more favourable relational-to-resistance balance preceded higher commercial-receptivity talk (association, lagged).
  • Normalization occurred without a matching rise in positive sentiment — audiences treat the VI as an ordinary influencer, including complaining about her ads.
  • The crossover point is a practical leading indicator for timing promotional intensity, to be read with its interval.

You cannot say

  • That individual consumers changed their minds (different people comment each week) — unless Layer B is strong, and then only as supporting evidence.
  • That the crossover causes purchase intention or sales.
  • That the uncanny valley is “temporary and self-decaying” in general — repeated exposure lowers eeriness, but liking depends on the persona's behaviour (Złotowski et al., 2015).
  • Anything about Instagram/TikTok audiences — this is Facebook Vietnam.

Managerial implications you will be able to write: (1) a measurable readiness signal for when to shift a VI from awareness/character-building content to product integration; (2) which aspects (appearance, voice, persona, disclosure) drive resistance at each stage, i.e. where to invest; (3) evidence that character/relational content — not rendering quality alone — is what moves the audience toward commercial receptivity; (4) that AI disclosure and sponsorship density moderate the curves.
